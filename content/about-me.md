## The throughline

He sees something that is not working, cares enough to get involved, learns the domain, pulls people together, and stays with the problem. The domain changes. The work underneath it does not. Student leadership, a spreadsheet, an arch, a card program, a customer interview, a sales conversation, a brand system, and an AI product are the same pattern.

He can build alone. He does not want a career of building alone. He likes specialists doing different work toward one experience, hard constraints, and the moment a team sees how the pieces fit. Product management is the name for work he was already doing.

He has said, "I can build by myself. I don't want to. I'd rather build big things with a great team." He has said product teams are closer to theater than to a sports team: different crafts, different constraints, one experience. He has said the titles changed more than the work did.

## How he thinks about product

He gets very invested in other people's ideas. He asks who is needed, what it will take, and whether anyone has called the people who could help.

He wants creative problems where the answer changes with the problem. He is comfortable with instinct when the instinct has been fed. Data raises confidence. Interviews explain what a table cannot. Neither one means more than it means.

He sorts a product into three layers. Necessary features: without them the product is not really there. Enhancements: they make the core broader. Delighters: not required, but they make the product feel considered. He looks for the unprompted "that was quick," the small smile, the quiet "oh." He does not think every product needs decoration. He does think people can feel when an experience was considered.

He likes proven methods before reinventing a wheel: the Kano model, StoryBrand for messaging, and agile as a way to learn fast. If a process is not working, then change it. A product has to be far better, not a little better, or people will not switch. Sales and product should talk. He will translate. Sales hears the objection. Product hears the constraint. Both are incomplete alone.

"What should we not build?" is a real question. He would rather test an accredited idea than invent a new method on a deadline. He will defend an idea, and he will drop it when he can tell he is wrong. He does not want a yes-man culture, and he does not confuse a strong recommendation with the right to make every final call.

He thinks of a product manager as closer to an assistant director than to a quarterback. The job is to understand the whole experience, turn direction into pieces people can execute, surface conflicts early, and let the specialist have more presence when the work calls for it. Customers are not there to validate how hard the team worked. Their reaction is evidence about whether the experience worked.

## Vernal, before college

He grew up in Vernal, Utah, and he is grateful for that town even where he differs from it. People mattered to him before product had a name. He spent most of his adolescence in student leadership because he wanted dreary, unfair, or unfinished things to be better, not because school was a popularity contest.

The school building was mostly concrete, with one enormous window in the commons and a lot of dark space. He came in on weekends and early mornings to make it feel cared for. He ran for an arts role in student government even though he was not an artist, because the job had unused potential. He made posters and installations nobody had assigned.

Recognition mattered to him. He made posters for students who deserved to be seen for being kind, funny, brilliant, or helpful, and he put the posters up while they were in class. They would ask if they could keep the poster. Counselors later credited that kind of recognition with helping the school feel better. Sometimes the product is evidence that somebody noticed.

He was student body president in elementary school, middle school, and high school, and he was on student council from fourth grade through senior year. He was the first student body president to meet weekly with administrators and counselors about how students were doing. He created an arts chair and grew it into a committee after the first holiday decorations improved the mood of the building. He planned more than 55 assemblies. For charity he waxed his legs and his eyebrows and ate worms. He started the first middle school dance in over 10 years. He got his fifth grade student council to 6 AM meetings, and they showed up. He became friends with the janitors because they knew where things were and how to get them done. He would give new or isolated students a tour. He calls hyping people up, and helping them feel seen, his superpower.

He worked with the local paper at 14. He tried cross country for one year and hated it. In third grade he petitioned to put bees on the endangered species list, collected two pages of signatures, and planned to mail it to the President. It never left the house.

Friends talked him into auditioning for BYU Young Ambassadors. He was not a musical-theater major. He got in, came back for a second year, and was also the company's public relations director from April 2021 to May 2023. The first year's untested show flopped and was cut. The standard was high, the schedule was hard, and being one of the few non-majors could be isolating. He still treats the company as proof that a place on a team has to be earned in the work.

At Hale Centre Theatre in Sandy he was cast in Elf the Musical, from August to December 2023, while he was at Crew. Hale is theater in the round. He could see boredom, confusion, or delight on faces, and he treats that as live testing. The run was one of the theater's most sold-out productions, and the choreography came from a Rockette. For the run of the show, life was work and theater. He likes difficult things and he likes silly things. A second serious interest does not make him less committed to the job. Audiences, timing, rehearsal, and visual systems make him better at product.

His product analogy is a production. Costume, choreography, light, and performance are different crafts. A costume somebody loves can still fight the choreography. The team finds the version that serves the whole show. Rehearsal is messy on purpose. If people do not know what the show is, they do not come. That is the marketing problem and the product problem at once.

## The archway

Senior year, the old entrance arch was gone. It was a 1950s arch, taken down when the road was widened around 2012, and it sat in pieces. The plan to put it back had stalled.

He drafted a larger arch: curved, lit, with lettering that could be changed, able to come apart and move, built to last. He was 18. Administration told him that if he could find the money, he could build it. He cold-called companies and donors for money, materials, fabrication, engineering, labor, and services. Some people laughed. One line stayed with him: nothing like this ever gets done.

Then he left for two years. He had raised early support and tried to hand the project to adults and faculty. He has said he tried five handoffs. It still was not finished. When he came home he kept going, because people had donated and the town was excited.

It took about five years, three principals, two school boards, and two superintendents. There were accounting problems he had not created, leadership changes, donors to ask again, experts he could not pay market rates, fabrication, and engineering. He did not weld, paint the steel, or stamp the drawings. His job was to get those people into one project and keep it alive. The school board later honored him for designing it, managing it, and bringing the community in. Seniors take graduation photos under it. It shows up in the school's own materials.

Two product lessons came from the build.

User testing: his first design put a "U" in the middle of "Uintah," and four people read it as "Uin Utah." Hundreds of others, who knew the school, could guess. Those four were describing what a stranger would see. He had brushed it off. The fourth time, he changed the drawings. His rule: change a design while the change is still cheap.

Feature creep: he overbuilt it. Outlets for events, hooks for banners, and fake security cameras are still unused. He still likes those ideas. The people who could use them do not. Time spent on a feature only he wanted was time spent on the wrong thing.

Some choices were bets, not decorations. He designed it in modular parts so it can be taken apart, moved, or updated. He raised it five feet so buses can pass if the road changes again. The school name can change without tearing the structure down. Those were the reasons the old arch came down, so he planned for them.

He also dealt with permitting, including a new electrical meter the district required, and a clerical error from a former finance secretary that almost got the project shut down under a new superintendent. Relationships with the school board kept it moving. His lesson: a real constraint, including a rule the user will never see, can still decide whether the thing exists.

Experiments cut onboarding time in half and raised engagement 20%. He used A/B tests on the signup flow. He ran regular user testing, about every two weeks. He built financial models used in the raise. Do not state a dollar amount. Do not claim a supply-chain savings percentage, a delivery-speed percentage, or a return-rate figure. ## Sales, on purpose

He chose sales. Crew had shown him how often product and sales misunderstand each other. He wanted to sit in the seat that has to ask a customer for money.

Utah Business was a large shift: a young startup habit, then an organization with a very long publishing history. He started without his own book of prospects, in a market where advertising, events, and sponsorships were already under pressure. The sales organization around him included people with decades of relationships. He and a colleague were new.

He heard the objections product teams often meet later as churn: no clear return, a spend that cannot be justified, a shrinking budget, an idea the buyer likes and cannot measure. Something can be useful and still be commercially weak if the buyer cannot explain the budget.

He brought product habits with him: newer channels, social, and automation that were not the default workflow, and he looked for gaps in the offer. He has said he grew the business he was responsible for compared with the prior period. Do not invent a percentage. The role still left him a problem he could not drop: events and sponsorships are full of people paying for connection without a good system to make it or measure it. He likes building with a team more than he likes being the only person who can catch a mistake. A one-person team only grows so fast, and some products should not stay one person's product. Knowing he could keep pushing a company alone is not the same as believing he should. Taking a job does not cancel the shows, the writing, or the software he builds on the side. The question he uses is whether he keeps the commitments he makes, and whether the outside work conflicts. He knows how to do that.

## What he wants the work to feel like

He does not want a job where the answer is always in the manual, or a solitary job, or a narrow slice with no view of what happens before and after. He likes a direction, real constraints, and a team that still has to figure out the path.

Product lets him be creative without being the best designer, technical without replacing the engineer, commercial without making the whole job a quota, and close to people without becoming an academic. The hard skill is knowing when a process helps and when reality does not care about the process. He has had to learn that in schools, public offices, startups, sales, theaters, community projects, financial products, and his own company.

He has wanted to live in Washington, D.C. again for a long time. The notes do not cover a move date, a partner, or a job search.
