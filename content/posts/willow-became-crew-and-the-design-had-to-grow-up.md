Let’s talk about turning a family-banking startup called Willow into Crew, and how the name change influenced the product’s visual identity.

The company I joined was called Willow. The company people eventually knew was Crew.

Those names carry slightly different pictures in my head.

Willow was the early product: an ambitious family bank, a premium credit concept, interest rates, rewards, and a lot of things we wanted to make possible.

Crew became a much broader design problem. How do you build a banking brand that families can recognize themselves in without making it look like a children's app?

## Start with the premise

Our original Willow investor deck was explicit about family banking. It included checking accounts for family members, youth features, financial literacy, and a premium card that could help people build credit.

It was a coherent pitch. It was also very much a pitch. We were learning what the banking infrastructure would allow, what the economics could support, and what families cared about enough to switch.

The brand was changing alongside the product.

I remember discussing the name Crew and what it could signal: the people you're doing life with. The exact trademark explanation for moving away from Willow is still unverified. I don't want to create a tidy naming legend I can't back up.

## How do you draw a family?

I worked on brand art and illustrations, and we explored different directions with designers.

One 2023 visual deck credited to Adam Eastburn compared storybook-like illustrations, looser classic styles, and an animal-based approach. The deck explicitly said the images weren't final.

The question was bigger than which illustration style looked cutest. I didn't want a family banking experience that only reflected one kind of family.

You can show a bicycle, a trip, a shared meal, a place, a memory. You can talk about being together without making every screen feature the same stock-photo parents and two kids.

I used an iPad to make artwork, too. A historical résumé says I created 52 illustrations for the brand. I want to recover the source files and exact attribution before I treat that as a published production count.

## Banking still needs to feel like banking

There's a fine line between approachable and unserious when you're asking someone to put their savings into a new institution.

You need clarity around interest, withdrawals, account ownership, balances, and security. You also want people to feel that the product was built for humans who live complicated lives.

This was one of the reasons I liked working on the physical card and packaging at the same time as the app experience. The brand couldn't stop at the website. It had to survive the card arriving in the mail and someone opening an account for their kid.

We tried a number of illustration styles, card designs, and packaging ideas. I tested pages with parents and kept coming back to what made the experience understandable.

Sometimes a beautifully composed screen is still confusing. The customer doesn't owe you patience because you spent a week adjusting the artwork.

## The brand was also a business bet

We could design the most delightful family bank in the country. That didn't necessarily mean people would move their paycheck to it.

I kept asking what would make the product useful enough to switch. I was increasingly interested in money organization and digital pockets because they could become part of everyday financial routines.

That tension shaped how I looked at the brand. I wanted the identity to be warm and recognizable, but I also wanted the promise to be concrete.

## What I remember about building it

It was exhausting in the way work gets exhausting when a small group is making decisions that specialists normally make in several departments.

I was thinking about card materials and production one day, UX illustrations the next, and customer trust the day after that.

I never thought that was random. To me, it was all the same product. It was a person deciding whether they would trust us with their money, then encountering our product repeatedly in everyday life.

I liked that challenge more than I can probably explain. Even now, I see the old Willow deck and want to open the design files again.
