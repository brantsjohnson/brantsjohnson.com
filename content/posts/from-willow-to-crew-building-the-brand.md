The pitch was ambitious: family banking, high-yield accounts, premium cards, children's money experiences, and a product that could help families handle their finances together. The name felt soft and familiar. Then we became Crew.

A name change seems easy when you're reading it in a press release. It's a much bigger question when you're still trying to decide what the company should feel like.

I helped build the brand, visual system and product experience while we were figuring that out. Those are some of the pieces of work I still think about the most.

## There's a problem with drawing a family

We were building a financial product for families. So what should the website show?

It's tempting to draw the conventional family: two parents, two children, everyone smiling. But the people who share money, raise children, care for relatives or just build a life together don't always fit that picture.

I wanted the illustrations to feel like belonging without requiring everyone to look like they lived in the same stock photograph.

We explored more universal scenes: objects, activities, memories, places, movement. A family could recognize itself in the experience without being told what a family had to look like.

At the same time, it was a bank. It needed to be trusted with real money. We couldn't make the product feel like a children's game simply because children were one of the users.

That balance influenced everything: the illustration language, the web pages, onboarding, physical cards and packaging.

## I was drawing while learning the product

I learned a lot of the visual work by doing it. I used my iPad, made sketches, worked through illustration directions and kept revising until the pieces felt like they belonged together.

I designed dozens of illustrations and contributed to the visual experience across the product. My contemporaneous résumé records 52 illustrations and 10 animations from the period. Those assets mattered because a young financial brand has to explain complicated things without making people feel stupid.

We also explored different design styles with creative collaborators. Adam Eastburn's early direction deck, for example, presented storybook, classic and animal concepts. A style exploration is useful because you can compare more than pretty images. You can ask how the direction behaves across a long website, an onboarding flow, a physical card, a children's screen and a family account dashboard.

The more places a brand lives, the more expensive inconsistency becomes.

## The homepage was a hypothesis

I redesigned the homepage repeatedly and tested ideas with parents at a local shopping center.

I wanted to know what people understood in the first few seconds. Did they recognize Crew as a real financial product? Did they understand the family controls? Could they see why they should trust it? Did they care enough to start signing up?

I had to listen when people weren't as interested in a feature as we were.

That meant the brand work wasn't separate from product discovery. Every headline, illustration and screen was a chance to test what people thought we were offering.

And while all this was happening, the underlying product was changing. We started with a premium credit-card concept and eventually moved toward debit. The brand had to grow with the reality of what we could deliver.

## Crew gave us more room

I liked the flexibility of the name Crew. It could mean a household, a group of friends, a team or the people you do life with. There's something more expansive about that than simply pointing to the nuclear family.

The way I thought about the visual identity reflected that. I wanted a brand that felt warm, a little playful and recognizably ours, while still taking the responsibility of handling people's finances seriously.

There were a lot of tiny decisions in that work. How far can an illustration lean into whimsy before it damages credibility? How should the physical card relate to the app? What colors can work for both a parent and a child? Can the homepage explain the idea without making people read a novel?

I spent a significant part of my time trying to answer those questions with actual design work.

It's gratifying to look back at a product and recognize pieces of yourself in the way it looks, speaks and behaves. I can still see the sketches, the discarded layouts and the versions that took another attempt before they worked.
